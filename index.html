Atoms are neutral, yet they contain electrons

  • By the nineteenth century, enough evidence had piled up in favour of the atomic hypothesis of matter.
  • In 1897, J. J. Thomson (English physicist, 1856–1940) carried out experiments on electric discharge through gases.
  • They revealed that atoms of different elements contain negatively charged constituents (electrons), and these are identical for all atoms.
  • However, atoms as a whole are electrically neutral.
  • So an atom must also contain some positive charge to neutralise the negative charge of its electrons.
  • The big question: how are the positive charge and the electrons arranged inside the atom? In other words, what is the structure of an atom?

Thomson’s plum pudding model (1898)

  • The first model of the atom was proposed by J. J. Thomson in 1898.
  • The positive charge of the atom is uniformly distributed throughout the volume of the atom.
  • The negatively charged electrons are embedded in it, like seeds in a watermelon.
  • This picture was called, picturesquely, the plum pudding model.
  • Subsequent studies, described in this chapter, showed that the distribution of electrons and positive charge is very different from this model.
J. J. Thomson (1856–1940) · English physicist · his 1897 experiments showed electrons are part of every atom · Rutherford was his research student.

Light from matter: continuous or discrete?

Dense matter
  • Condensed matter (solids and liquids) and dense gases emit electromagnetic radiation at all temperatures.
  • The radiation has a continuous distribution of several wavelengths, with different intensities.
  • It is due to the oscillations of atoms and molecules, governed by the interaction of each atom or molecule with its neighbours.
Rarefied gas
  • In contrast, light from rarefied gases heated in a flame, or excited electrically in a glow tube (a neon sign, a mercury vapour light), has only certain discrete wavelengths.
  • The spectrum appears as a series of bright lines.
  • The average spacing between atoms is large, so the radiation is due to individual atoms, not to interactions between atoms or molecules.

Every element has its own line spectrum

  • In the early nineteenth century it was established that each element has a characteristic spectrum.
  • For example, hydrogen always gives a set of lines with fixed relative positions between the lines.
  • This suggested an intimate relationship between the internal structure of an atom and the spectrum it emits.
  • In 1885, Johann Jakob Balmer (1825–1898) obtained a simple empirical formula for the wavelengths of a group of lines emitted by atomic hydrogen.
  • Hydrogen is the simplest of the elements, so its spectrum is studied in detail later in this chapter.
Empirical means the formula was fitted to measured wavelengths. It worked, but nobody yet knew why.

Rutherford finds the nucleus

  • Ernest Rutherford (1871–1937), a former research student of J. J. Thomson, was working on α-particles emitted by some radioactive elements.
  • In 1906, he proposed a classic experiment: the scattering of α-particles by atoms, to investigate the atomic structure.
  • It was performed around 1911 by Hans Geiger (1882–1945) and Ernest Marsden (1889–1970), who was only 20 years old and had not yet earned his bachelor’s degree.
  • The details are in Section 12.2. The results led to Rutherford’s planetary model (the nuclear model) of the atom.
  • The entire positive charge and most of the mass are concentrated in a small volume called the nucleus.
  • The electrons revolve around the nucleus, just as planets revolve around the Sun.

Ernest Rutherford

1871 – 1937
  • Born in New Zealand; a British physicist who did pioneering work on radioactive radiation.
  • Discovered α-rays and β-rays.
  • With Frederick Soddy, created the modern theory of radioactivity.
  • Studied the “emanation” of thorium and discovered a new noble gas, an isotope of radon, now called thoron.
  • By scattering α-rays from metal foils, discovered the atomic nucleus and proposed the planetary model. He also estimated the approximate size of the nucleus.

The planetary model: a big step, but not enough

  • Rutherford’s nuclear model was a major step towards how we see the atom today.
  • However, it could not explain why atoms emit light of only discrete wavelengths.
  • How can an atom as simple as hydrogen, with a single electron and a single proton, emit a complex spectrum of specific wavelengths?
  • In the classical picture of an atom, the electron revolves round the nucleus like a planet revolves round the Sun.
  • But there are some serious difficulties in accepting such a model. The chapter takes these up next.
